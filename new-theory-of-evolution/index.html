Evolution and abiogenesis: the anthropic principle

“The world requires at least ten years to understand a new idea, however important or simple it may be.”

Sir Ronald Ross, The Prevention of Malaria (1910)

There was a shift in understanding cosmology at the beginning of the 21st centure: from a vast, but still finite-volume (sphere-like) universe, we switched to the much simpler flat 3-dimensional space, extentending to infinity in all directions.

This notion has bearings on the theory of evolution and abiogenesis: if there are infinite number of galaxies and Earth-like planets, any low-probability event (well, with some restrictions, explained later) will occur someone.

As we, humans, can only observe from a planet that managed to jump all these obstacles, we’ll have a distorted view of these events: seeing them as natural occurrences, even though they are highly improbable, very lucky coincidences.

This distortion must be taken into consideration, when discussion abiogenesis and evolution.

For evolution, anthropic selection may replace natural selection, and we may explain saltational speciation by an extremely ‘lucky’ series of mutations in the non-coding DNA, that creates new genes and control regions.

Even though the probability of accumulating these very precise mutations is practically zero, they will occur somewhere in the infinite universe.

Abiogenesis has a similar explanation: the first cells might have been formed by random synthesis of all the needed DNA, RNA, and proteins, over mineral surfaces acting as both catalysts and chaperones.

So LUCA (the Last Universal Common Ancestor) may have been developed in a single, unlikely step, without any evolution, or going through stages like the RNA world.
