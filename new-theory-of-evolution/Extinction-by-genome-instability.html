Genomic Instability as an Intrinsic Extinction Clock: A Hypothesis on Epigenetic Reboot Failure Driven by Non-Coding Drift

Abstract

Classical evolutionary paradigms attribute species extinction primarily to extrinsic drivers, such as competitive exclusion (Gause’s Law) or environmental catastrophes. Here, we propose an intrinsic, genome-wide mechanism of extinction rooted in developmental epigenetics and population genetics.

Following fertilization, the eukaryotic zygote undergoes extensive epigenetic reprogramming—an epigenetic “bootup”—erasing parental marks to re-establish open/closed chromatin domains, pericentromeric heterochromatin, and kinetochore structures. This architectural resetting depends not merely on protein-coding sequences, but on the structural integrity of the entire non-coding genome.

We hypothesize that the rapid, unconstrained spread of silent and near-neutral mutations across non-coding regions eventually reaches a critical threshold, destabilizing the sequence motifs required for zygotic reprogramming. Because neutral mutations diffuse rapidly across populations via genetic drift, this mutational threshold may be reached near-simultaneously across individuals, precipitating sudden, intrinsic population extinction without external ecological triggers.

1. Introduction: Beyond Extrinsic Extinction Paradigms

For decades, evolutionary theory has framed extinction as a consequence of external pressures. The competitive exclusion principle (Gause, 1934) posits that two species competing for the exact same limited resource cannot coexist indefinitely, leading to the competitive displacement of the less fit species.

Alternatively, macroevolutionary extinction events are frequently attributed to physical catastrophes, such as bolide impacts or abrupt climate shifts.

However, ecological observations increasingly document high species diversity in stable niches that standard competitive exclusion models struggle to explain (e.g., the “paradox of the plankton”). Furthermore, fossil records occasionally show abrupt lineage collapses lacking obvious environmental drivers.

We propose that living species possess an intrinsic, sequence-encoded expiration date driven by the limits of epigenetic fidelity during early development.

2. The Zygotic “Bootup” and Non-Coding Genome Architecture

Immediately following fertilization, the eukaryotic embryo undergoes a fundamental transition: parental epigenetic marks (such as DNA methylation and specific histone modifications) are globally erased and reset to establish totipotency.

This zygotic “bootup” process requires:

  1. Chromatin Domain Organization: Re-establishing higher-order chromatin structures, including topologically associating domains (TADs) and euchromatin/heterochromatin boundaries.

  2. Kinetochore and Centromere Assembly: Recruiting centromeric proteins (such as CENP-A) to specific pericentromeric repeat arrays and satellite DNA sequences.

  3. Zygotic Genome Activation (ZGA): Correctly timing the onset of transcription.

Crucially, this structural re-organization relies heavily on non-coding DNA, which constitutes the vast majority of complex eukaryotic genomes. Non-coding elements—including satellite repeats, transposable element remnants, and long non-coding RNA loci—provide the sequence scaffold for chromatin-binding complexes and structural nuclear positioning.

3. Neutral Drift and Population-Wide Epigenetic Breakdown

According to Motoo Kimura’s Neutral Theory of Molecular Evolution (Kimura, 1968), the vast majority of genetic substitutions at the molecular level are selectively neutral or nearly neutral. Because non-coding regions experience weaker purifying selection than protein-coding exons, silent and non-coding mutations accumulate and fix within populations at accelerated rates.

While individual non-coding substitutions may exert negligible effects on organismal fitness during adult life, their cumulative loss of structural information eventually degrades the regulatory landscape required for zygotic reprogramming:

  • Cumulative Degeneracy: Over evolutionary time, accumulated neutral mutations alter the thermodynamic stability, secondary structures, and protein-binding affinities of non-coding RNA and satellite repeats.

  • Bootup Failure: When non-coding sequence divergence crosses a structural threshold, the newly formed zygote becomes incapable of properly assembling kinetochores or re-establishing global chromatin architecture post-fertilization, resulting in early embryonic lethality.

  • Population-Wide Synchronization: Because neutral substitutions diffuse throughout an interbreeding population via genetic drift, individuals across the species accumulate these background non-coding mutations at comparable rates. As a result, the critical threshold of epigenetic instability is reached across the entire population within a narrow evolutionary timeframe, precipitating sudden, intrinsic extinction.

Testable Predictions

  1. Lineage-Age Correlated ZGA Failure: Older, monophyletic evolutionary lineages should display increased rates of post-fertilization embryonic failure that cannot be explained by coding-sequence mutations.

  2. Non-Coding Sequence Degeneracy Thresholds: Comparative genomics across extinct and extant sister taxa should reveal a non-linear threshold in non-coding/satellite sequence entropy beyond which zygotic chromatin assembly fails.

  3. In Vitro Epigenetic Rescue: Synthetically restoring ancestral non-coding sequence scaffolds in the zygote of a declining lineage should rescue early mitotic division and kinetochore assembly.
